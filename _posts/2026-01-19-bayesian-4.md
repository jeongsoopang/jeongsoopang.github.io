## 1) Overview

This post extends prior concepts into **improper**, **noninformative**, **Jeffreys**, **weakly informative**, and **reference priors**, and explains why **parameterization** changes what a “flat prior” really means.

---

## 2) Categories of Priors

| Type of Prior | Definition / Concept | Key Properties & Purpose | Typical Example / Use Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Conjugate Prior** | Prior family that yields a posterior in the *same distribution family* as the prior. | Closed-form posterior; simple hyperparameter updates. | Beta–Binomial, Gamma–Poisson, Normal–Normal |
| **Improper Prior** | Prior that does **not integrate to 1**, but may still yield a proper posterior. | Useful when little info exists; must check posterior propriety. | Flat prior $\pi(\mu)\propto 1$ for Normal mean |
| **Informative Prior** | Encodes strong domain knowledge. | Low variance; can dominate likelihood when data are scarce. | Disease rate based on previous clinical studies |
| **Weakly Informative Prior** | Proper but vague prior that gently regularizes. | Stabilizes inference without overwhelming the data. | $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$ or $\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$ for Binomial $\theta$ |
| **Noninformative Prior** | Tries to let likelihood dominate inference. | No universal form; depends on parameterization. | $\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ for Binomial $\theta$ |
| **Reference Prior** | Maximizes expected information gain from data. | “Objective Bayes” choice; useful in multi-parameter models. | Reference priors for nuisance parameters |
| **Jeffreys Prior** | $\pi(\theta)\propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}$ using Fisher information. | Invariant under reparameterization. | For Bernoulli $p$: $\pi(p)\propto [p(1-p)]^{-1/2}$ (Beta$(1/2,1/2)$) |
| **Invariant / Nonparametric Prior** | Priors over transformations or infinite-dimensional spaces. | Flexible modeling (GP, DP, hierarchical Bayes). | Gaussian Process, Dirichlet Process |

> Table math uses `$...$` to avoid rendering issues in Minimal Mistakes.

## 3) Proper vs. Improper Priors

A prior $p(\theta)$ is **proper** if it integrates to 1:

$$
\int p(\theta)\,d\theta = 1
$$

Otherwise it is **improper**:

$$
\int p(\theta)\,d\theta = \infty
$$

Key rule:
- An improper prior is acceptable **only if** the resulting posterior is **proper**.
- If the posterior is also improper, the prior must be discarded.

### Example 1: Binomial + $\mathrm{Beta}(0,0)$

For $y\sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta)$ and prior $\theta\sim \mathrm{Beta}(0,0)$,

the posterior is:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(y,\;n-y)
$$

This posterior is proper when $y>0$ and $n-y>0$.

### Example 2: Normal mean with flat prior

For $X_i\sim \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ (with $\sigma^2$ known) and $\pi(\mu)\propto 1$:

$$
\mu \mid x \sim \mathcal N(\bar{x},\sigma^2/n)
$$

So the flat prior still yields a proper posterior here.

## 4) Informative vs. Noninformative Priors

- **Informative prior:** low variance, strong prior knowledge.
- **Diffuse / vague prior:** high variance, weak information.
- **Flat prior:** constant over parameter space (e.g., $\pi(\theta)\propto 1$).

**Noninformative priors**
- Aim to let data “speak for itself.”
- Used in **objective Bayes** when prior knowledge is limited.
- Have no universal definition: the form depends on parameterization.

---

## 5) Parameterization Sensitivity

A prior that is “flat” in one parameterization may be highly informative in another.

---

### 5.1 Example 1: Binomial with flat prior on $\theta$

For $y\sim \mathrm{Bin}(n,\theta)$:

- Flat prior: $\theta\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$ (equivalently $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$)

Posterior:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(y+1,\;n-y+1)
$$

---

### 5.2 Example 2: Odds transformation

Let $o=\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}$ and assume a flat prior on odds: $p(o)\propto 1$.

The induced prior on $\theta$ becomes:

$$
p(\theta)\propto \frac{1}{(1-\theta)^2}, \quad 0<\theta<1
$$

This favors $\theta$ near 1 and is **improper** since:

$$
\int_0^1 (1-\theta)^{-2}\,d\theta=\infty
$$

Posterior under Binomial likelihood:

$$
p(\theta\mid y)\propto \theta^y (1-\theta)^{n-y-2}
\Rightarrow
\theta \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(y+1,\;n-y-1)
$$

Posterior becomes improper when $n-y\le 1$.

---

### 5.3 Example 3: Log-odds reparameterization

Let $\rho=\log\!\left(\frac{\theta}{1-\theta}\right)$ and assume $\rho\sim \mathrm{Uniform}(-\infty,\infty)$.

Then the induced prior on $\theta$ is:

$$
p(\theta)\propto \frac{1}{\theta(1-\theta)}
$$

Posterior becomes:

$$
\theta \mid y \sim \mathrm{Beta}(y,\;n-y)
$$

**Insight:** “Flat” depends on the scale.  
Uniform in $\theta$, odds $o$, or log-odds $\rho$ implies very different beliefs.

---

## 6) Jeffreys’ Prior

Motivation: “uninformative” priors should behave consistently under smooth reparameterization.

Jeffreys’ rule:

$$
\pi(\theta)\propto \sqrt{I(\theta)}
$$

where $I(\theta)$ is **Fisher information**:

$$
I(\theta)=\mathbb E_\theta\!\left[\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\log L(\theta;X)\right)^2\right]
$$

Interpretation:
- Higher prior density where the likelihood is more sensitive to $\theta$.
- Determined purely by the likelihood (objective construction).
- Invariant to reparameterization.

---

### 6.1 Example 1: Bernoulli

For $X_i\sim \mathrm{Bern}(p)$:

$$
I(p)=\frac{n}{p(1-p)}
\Rightarrow
\pi(p)\propto [p(1-p)]^{-1/2}
$$

So Jeffreys prior is:

$$
p \sim \mathrm{Beta}\!\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)
$$

---

### 6.2 Example 2: Normal mean

For $X\sim \mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ (with known $\sigma^2$):

$$
I(\mu)=\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\Rightarrow
\pi(\mu)\propto 1
$$

So Jeffreys prior for $\mu$ is uniform.

---

## 7) Weakly Informative Priors

Weakly informative priors are **proper but vague** priors that help:

- regularize estimates,
- avoid improper posteriors,
- reduce instability in small samples.

Example choices for Binomial $\theta$:
- $\mathrm{Beta}(1,1)$
- $\mathrm{Beta}(1/2,1/2)$

These behave similarly to aggressive improper choices (like $\mathrm{Beta}(0,0)$) but keep posteriors well-defined.

---

## 8) Reference Priors

A **reference prior** maximizes expected information gain between data and parameters:

$$
p^*(\theta)
=
\arg\max_{p(\theta)}
I(\Theta, T)
$$

where mutual information is:

$$
I(\Theta, T)
=
\iint
p(\theta, t)\,
\log\!\frac{p(\theta, t)}{p(\theta)p(t)}\,d\theta\,dt
$$

Interpretation:
- objective choice designed to maximize what the data can teach us,
- especially useful in multi-parameter settings (with nuisance parameters).

---

## Key Takeaway

> “Uninformative” is not absolute — it depends on parameterization.  
> Jeffreys’ rule offers invariance, and weakly informative priors offer practical stability,  
> so inference is driven by data rather than arbitrary parameter choices.
