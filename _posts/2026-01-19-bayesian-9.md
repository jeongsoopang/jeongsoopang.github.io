## 1) Why Bayesian Computation

Bayesian inference is based on the posterior distribution:

$$
p(\theta \mid y)
=
\frac{p(y \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)}{p(y)}
$$

where the marginal likelihood

$$
p(y)
=
\int p(y \mid \theta)\,\pi(\theta)\,d\theta
$$

is often **intractable**, especially when:

- Parameter space is high-dimensional  
- No conjugate prior exists  
- Likelihood is complex or nonlinear  

Therefore, Bayesian analysis relies heavily on **computational methods**:

- Analytical solutions (rare)
- Numerical integration (low-dimensional only)
- Simulation-based methods (Monte Carlo, MCMC)

---

## 2) Monte Carlo Integration

Monte Carlo methods approximate expectations of the form:

$$
E[g(\theta)]
=
\int g(\theta)\,p(\theta \mid y)\,d\theta
$$

---

### 2.1 Monte Carlo Approximation

1. Draw samples  
   $$
   \theta^{(1)},\dots,\theta^{(N)} \sim p(\theta \mid y)
   $$

2. Approximate the expectation:

$$
\widehat{E}[g(\theta)]
=
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^N g(\theta^{(i)})
$$

By the **Law of Large Numbers**:

$$
\widehat{E}[g(\theta)] \to E[g(\theta)]
\quad \text{as } N \to \infty
$$

---

## 3) Direct Sampling (Ideal Case)

If we can sample directly from $p(\theta \mid y)$, inference becomes trivial.

**Example: Normal–Normal conjugate model**

- Prior: $\theta \sim N(\mu_0,\tau_0^2)$  
- Likelihood: $y \mid \theta \sim N(\theta,\sigma^2)$  
- Posterior: $\theta \mid y \sim N(\mu_n,\tau_n^2)$  

✔ Direct sampling is possible.

**Limitation:**  
Most Bayesian models do **not** admit closed-form posteriors → need approximate methods.

---

## 4) Rejection Sampling

Goal: Sample from a target density $p(\theta)$ using an easier proposal $q(\theta)$.

---

### 4.1 Algorithm

Choose $q(\theta)$ and constant $M$ such that:

$$
p(\theta) \le M q(\theta)
\quad \text{for all } \theta
$$

Repeat:

1. Sample $\theta^\* \sim q(\theta)$  
2. Sample $u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$  
3. Accept $\theta^\*$ if:

$$
u < \frac{p(\theta^\*)}{M q(\theta^\*)}
$$

---

### 4.2 Pros and Cons

| Pros | Cons |
|---|---|
| Simple | Finding $M$ can be difficult |
| Exact samples | Inefficient in high dimensions |

---

## 5) Importance Sampling

Used to approximate expectations under $p(\theta)$ using samples from $q(\theta)$.

---

### Key Identity

$$
E_p[g(\theta)]
=
\int g(\theta)
\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}\,q(\theta)\,d\theta
$$

Approximation:

$$
E_p[g(\theta)]
\approx
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{i=1}^N g(\theta^{(i)})\,w(\theta^{(i)})
$$

where importance weights are:

$$
w(\theta)
=
\frac{p(\theta)}{q(\theta)}
$$

Normalized weights:

$$
\tilde{w}_i
=
\frac{w_i}{\sum_{j=1}^N w_j}
$$

**Requirement:**  
$q(\theta) > 0$ wherever $p(\theta) > 0$.

---

## 6) Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)

When direct sampling is impossible, we construct a **Markov chain** whose stationary distribution is $p(\theta \mid y)$.

Key ideas:

- Generate dependent samples  
- Discard early samples (burn-in)  
- Use remaining samples to approximate posterior quantities  

---

## 7) Metropolis–Hastings Algorithm

The **Metropolis–Hastings (MH)** algorithm is the most general MCMC method.

---

### 7.1 Algorithm

Goal: Construct a Markov chain $\{\theta^{(t)}\}$ with stationary distribution $p(\theta \mid y)$.

1. Initialize $\theta^{(0)}$
2. For iteration $t = 0,1,2,\dots$:

**(a) Propose**
$$
\theta^\* \sim q(\theta^\* \mid \theta^{(t)})
$$

**(b) Acceptance probability**
$$
\alpha(\theta^{(t)},\theta^\*)
=
\min\!\left(
1,\;
\frac{p(\theta^\*)\,q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^\*)}
     {p(\theta^{(t)})\,q(\theta^\* \mid \theta^{(t)})}
\right)
$$

**(c) Accept / Reject**

- With probability $\alpha$: $\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^\*$  
- Otherwise: $\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}$

---

### 7.2 Random Walk Metropolis

A common proposal:

$$
q(\theta^\* \mid \theta^{(t)})
=
N(\theta^{(t)},\sigma^2)
$$

Since the proposal is symmetric:

$$
q(\theta^\* \mid \theta^{(t)})
=
q(\theta^{(t)} \mid \theta^\*)
$$

Acceptance simplifies to:

$$
\alpha
=
\min\!\left(
1,\;
\frac{p(\theta^\*)}{p(\theta^{(t)})}
\right)
$$

---

## 8) Gibbs Sampling

A special MCMC method that samples from **full conditional distributions**.

---

### 8.1 When to Use

For multivariate parameters:

$$
\theta = (\theta_1,\theta_2,\dots,\theta_k)
$$

when each conditional distribution is tractable.

---

### 8.2 Algorithm

At iteration $t$:

1. Sample  
   $$
   \theta_1^{(t+1)}
   \sim
   p(\theta_1 \mid \theta_2^{(t)},\dots,\theta_k^{(t)})
   $$
2. Sample  
   $$
   \theta_2^{(t+1)}
   \sim
   p(\theta_2 \mid \theta_1^{(t+1)},\theta_3^{(t)},\dots)
   $$
3. Continue until $\theta_k^{(t+1)}$

---

### 8.3 Advantages

- Always accepts proposed values  
- Very efficient for conjugate and hierarchical models  

---

## 9) Convergence Diagnostics

To assess MCMC reliability:

| Diagnostic | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Trace plot | Visual inspection of mixing |
| Autocorrelation | Measure dependence between samples |
| Effective Sample Size (ESS) | Equivalent number of independent samples |
| Gelman–Rubin $\hat{R}$ | Check convergence across multiple chains |

---

## Key Takeaway

- Bayesian computation enables inference beyond conjugate models  
- Monte Carlo approximates expectations via sampling  
- MCMC constructs samples when direct sampling fails  
- Metropolis–Hastings and Gibbs are foundational algorithms  
- Diagnostics are essential for trustworthy inference
